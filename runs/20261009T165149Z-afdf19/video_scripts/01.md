**Narration:**
A data-center site can look promising on a map: land is available, a transmission line runs nearby, and generation is somewhere in the region. But none of that, by itself, tells a developer how much electricity the site can actually receive—or when.

For AI infrastructure, the sharper real-estate question is not just, “Is power nearby?” It’s: “What capacity can be delivered to this specific site, on what schedule, and at what cost?” Recent grid and storage developments make that distinction especially important.

**On-screen text:** “Nearby power ≠ deliverable power”

**Narration:**
One recent example shows why timing matters. PJM accepted three projects totaling about 2.1 gigawatts into its Expedited Interconnection Track. They include two planned storage projects from Engie IR Holdings and a gas-fired uprate proposed by an LS Power unit. Utility Dive reported the development; PJM's own announcement confirms the more precise term, "accepted."

PJM expects those projects to come online in mid-2029. That is a concrete grid response, but it is still a future date. Acceptance into the track is not a final interconnection agreement or operating capacity—and planned capacity should not be counted as power already available to a data-center site.

The distinction is easy to miss when a headline gives a large number. For real-estate diligence, the schedule attached to the megawatts matters as much as the megawatts themselves. And because two projects are storage, there is a second question: can storage improve future power options without being mistaken for supply available at this site today?

## 1:35–2:35 — Storage economics, with a site-level caveat
**Scene direction:** Move from the PJM storage-project markers to a separate comparison graphic: a battery icon and gas peaker icon. Label the comparison “Wood Mackenzie estimate, reported by Utility Dive”; do not imply it explains PJM's acceptance of the projects.

**Narration:**
A separate cost comparison shows why storage deserves attention. Utility Dive reported Wood Mackenzie’s estimate that, for a 2026 U.S. commercial operation date, four-hour battery storage is 65 to 75 percent cheaper than new open-cycle gas turbine peakers, depending on state-level carbon pricing.

That is an attributed estimate, not a universal price guarantee. The report supplies neither the underlying model nor exact prices. A four-hour battery can provide power for four hours at its rated output; this comparison does not show that it can independently meet a data center's continuing demand.

So the two findings answer different questions. The cost estimate speaks to a possible way of adding capacity; PJM's expected 2029 date speaks to when particular projects might operate. Neither tells us that electricity is deliverable to a specific parcel today.

**Narration:**
A broker interviewed in October described power availability, how quickly it could be delivered, and the cost of bringing it to a site as key questions. The broker also pointed to zoning as a difficulty.

That is useful practitioner perspective, not a market-wide measurement or independent verification of project outcomes. Still, it reinforces a practical diligence checklist: What capacity is actually deliverable? What is the credible delivery timeline? What will it cost to bring power to the site? Commercial estimates, utility approvals, and practitioner accounts each tell us something different. None alone proves that a specific parcel is ready for an AI data center.
