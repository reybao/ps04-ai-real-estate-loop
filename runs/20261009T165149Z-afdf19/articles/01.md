# A Data Center Site Isn’t Powered Just Because Power Is Nearby

**As of October 9, 2026**

A parcel can sit near a transmission line, a power plant, or a proposed battery project and still lack the electricity a data center needs. For developers and investors assessing sites for AI computing, the critical question is not simply whether power exists in the region. It is whether enough power can be delivered to this particular site, on a useful schedule, and at a cost that works.

That distinction matters because data centers are built around large, continuing electricity needs. A nearby line is part of the grid, but proximity alone does not establish that the line or the wider system can serve a new facility. Nor does a utility application—the process of seeking a connection—mean that capacity has been granted or delivered. A speaker in a recent AI infrastructure keynote put it plainly: “A utility application is not delivered capacity.” [01-c2]

For real-estate diligence, that turns power into more than a box to check after land is selected. Developers need to understand the capacity available to the project, when it can be connected, and what it will cost to bring it there. A broker interviewed this month described availability, timing, and delivery cost as central site-selection questions, while also pointing to zoning as a challenge. That is a practitioner’s account, not a measurement of the whole market, but it captures the practical overlap between land use and infrastructure: a site must be legally usable and physically serviceable. [01-c3]

### New capacity is not immediate capacity

A recent development in the PJM Interconnection region illustrates the timing issue. PJM accepted three projects totaling about 2.1 gigawatts into its Expedited Interconnection Track: two planned storage projects and a proposed gas-fired upgrade. PJM expects the projects to come online in mid-2029. [01-c2]

A gigawatt is a unit of power; here, the figure describes the projects’ combined capacity. But the headline number should not be mistaken for electricity already flowing to a data center. Acceptance into the expedited track is a step in the interconnection process, not a final interconnection agreement or operating capacity. The expected online date is still in the future, so a developer cannot treat these planned projects as present-day supply. The retained media passage is secondary reporting; a subsequent human cross-check of PJM's announcement confirmed the narrower term, "accepted."

Two of the accepted projects are storage projects. That makes the PJM announcement a useful bridge to another question: even if storage becomes a more attractive way to add capacity, what does that say—and not say—about a particular site's power supply?

### Storage may improve the options, not prove site readiness

A separate cost comparison helps explain why storage deserves attention, though it does not explain PJM's decision on those specific projects. Utility Dive reported Wood Mackenzie’s estimate that, for a 2026 U.S. commercial operation date, four-hour battery storage is 65% to 75% cheaper than new open-cycle gas turbine peakers, depending on whether state-level carbon pricing applies. [01-c1]

A four-hour battery is a storage system described by how long it can provide power at its rated output. An open-cycle gas turbine peaker is a gas-fired plant designed to provide power during periods of high demand. The comparison suggests that batteries may be a competitive alternative for some grid needs. It does not establish that a battery can independently meet a particular data center’s requirements, that a project has access to one, or that the estimate applies to every location and contract.

The figure is an attributed Wood Mackenzie estimate reported by Utility Dive—not independently verified project economics. The supplied report provides no underlying model or exact prices. It compares two ways of meeting some grid needs; it does not compare powered and unpowered real-estate sites. The percentage should therefore inform questions about possible future supply, not substitute for a site-specific power assessment.

This is where real-estate underwriting can go wrong. Underwriting is the process of estimating a project's costs, risks, and expected returns. If a site's business case assumes power before delivery is credible, the project may face a gap between when buildings or computing equipment are ready and when the grid can serve them. The evidence here does not quantify that risk for any particular site; it shows why timing belongs in diligence, not just the engineering discussion.

The strongest counterpoint is that new projects, storage, and faster interconnection processes may expand the pool of power available to large loads. PJM’s acceptance of these proposals and Wood Mackenzie’s cost estimate are reasons not to assume conventional supply is the only route. But a lower estimated storage cost does not make PJM's planned projects operational today, and neither finding establishes delivery to a specific parcel. The grounded takeaway is narrower: assess power as a timed, priced service, not a nearby feature on a map. Ask what capacity can reach the parcel, when it can do so, and what evidence supports that schedule. [01-c1] [01-c2] [01-c3]
