# A data-center site is not a delivered AI campus

**As of October 10, 2026**

A proposed data-center campus can have a name, a site, a headline capacity figure and a power line nearby—and still not be ready to run a single AI workload. The distinction matters as real-estate professionals assess projects whose announcements can sound more complete than the underlying infrastructure.

A recent plan for the RELLIS site in Texas makes the gap concrete. Data Center Knowledge reports that ThisWay Global plans a phased Research and AI Compute Center totaling 73 megawatts (MW) on about 25 acres of Texas A&M University System land. A megawatt is a unit of power; here, 73 MW is the planned capacity figure, not evidence of power already being delivered to operating servers. [02-c1]

The company’s own description underscores that qualification. ThisWay Global says deployment will be tied to confirmed power availability and applicable utility approvals. That statement sets out conditions for the plan; it does not confirm that the power has been secured, that approvals have been granted, or when any phase will open. [02-c2] The reported development is therefore a proposal with a stated capacity target—not proof of 73 MW in service.

### Follow the path from site to service

For students and early-career practitioners, the useful question is not simply, “How large is the announced campus?” It is, “What has to happen before the site can serve its intended load?” A practical due-diligence framework follows several connected questions: Is the site and its buildings suitable for the proposed use? Is there a credible path to the amount of electricity needed, on a usable schedule and at a workable cost? Are utility and land-use approvals in place? Is financing available, and can the project be executed? These are linked requirements, not interchangeable signs of readiness.

A keynote speaker makes the power distinction bluntly: “A land near power transmission lines is not a powered site,” and “A utility application is not delivered capacity.” [02-c3] In plain terms, proximity to transmission infrastructure does not establish that a project can connect to it and receive the power it needs. Nor does asking a utility to assess or provide service mean the requested capacity is already available. The speaker’s further warning—that an announced campus is not necessarily an operating data center—supports the same distinction. [02-c5]

A data-center broker, discussing site evaluation, points to how much power is available, how quickly it can be obtained, and its cost; the broker also describes zoning as difficult. [02-c4] Zoning is the set of local rules governing what may be built or operated on a property. These comments are useful as a practitioner’s perspective on what may shape a site decision, not as a measured account of every market or project. A broker involved in data-center land transactions also has a commercial interest in this field.

### Reuse is an opportunity, not a shortcut

The RELLIS proposal concerns redevelopment of an unfinished project. Reusing an existing site may offer a different starting point from developing untouched land, but the available reporting does not establish that redevelopment removes the need to resolve power, approvals, financing or construction. The evidence also does not provide a complete project record—from acquisition through utility connection and energization—against which to judge how far the plan has progressed.

That is the central limit in interpreting the headline. The 73 MW figure and 25-acre site are reported elements of the company’s plan. The company’s power-and-approval condition is a statement about what deployment depends on. Neither, on the evidence available here, demonstrates delivered capacity. [02-c1] [02-c2] Practitioner commentary reinforces the distinction between indicators of a possible site and actual service, but it does not independently audit RELLIS. [02-c3] [02-c5]

There is a fair counterpoint: plans and utility applications are necessary steps, and a phased proposal may be a meaningful development milestone even before a facility operates. But milestones should be named accurately. A land position, proposed megawatts, nearby grid infrastructure, an application, an approval, a completed connection and operating capacity describe different stages. Collapsing them into one label—“AI campus”—can obscure what remains unresolved.

For real-estate, architecture and engineering teams, the practical lesson is to ask which stage each cited number or milestone represents, who is making the claim, and what evidence confirms the next step. The supplied sources do not establish a universal project timeline or prove that RELLIS will, or will not, reach operation. They do support a narrower conclusion: announced capacity is not delivered capacity, and a site becomes usable for AI only as the necessary pieces line up. [02-c5]
